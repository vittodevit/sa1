Understanding AI risks

Artificial intelligence refers to computer systems that perform tasks such as recognising patterns, making predictions and generating content. Generative AI can produce text, images, audio and code in response to instructions. These capabilities have useful applications, but they can also introduce risks to cybersecurity and digital privacy.

AI-related risks can arise in different ways. An attacker may deliberately use AI to support a scam. A legitimate user may accidentally share confidential information with an AI service. An AI system may also produce an incorrect answer or be manipulated through malicious input.

It is therefore important to distinguish between the misuse of AI, weaknesses within AI systems and mistakes made when people rely on their outputs. The consequences depend on the information involved, the system's capabilities and the permissions it has been given.

How AI can support cyberattacks

AI can help attackers create convincing messages more quickly. Instead of writing every fraudulent email manually, an attacker may generate many variations, translate them into different languages and adapt them to specific audiences. This can increase the scale and personalisation of phishing.

Personal information published online can make these messages more persuasive. References to a person's university, workplace or interests may make a fraudulent request appear familiar. Correct grammar and a professional tone are not reliable evidence that a message is genuine.

An example of an AI-assisted scam

A student receives an email that appears to come from university support. It mentions the start of the semester and asks the student to verify their account through a link. The message looks professional, but the link leads to a fake login page designed to collect credentials. AI may help write the message, while the underlying attack remains a phishing attempt.

AI can also assist with generating or explaining code, including code that could be misused. However, generated code may contain errors, and an AI tool does not automatically make an attacker capable of compromising any system. Successful attacks still depend on factors such as vulnerabilities, access and technical knowledge.

Personal data and privacy

When someone enters information into an AI service, that information is processed by the system. Depending on the service and its settings, conversations may be stored or made available for additional processing. Users should understand how a service handles information before submitting sensitive content.

Uploading a document can disclose more than expected. A file may contain names, contact details, private messages or confidential organisational information. Removing a person's name may not fully protect their identity if other details still make them recognisable.

Profiling and inference

AI can identify patterns across large amounts of data. Combining browsing activity, purchases and location records may allow a system to infer interests, routines or sensitive characteristics. These inferences can affect privacy even when users never directly provided the inferred information. They may also be inaccurate.

Surveillance and identification

Facial recognition and other automated analysis tools can help identify or track people across images and video. Their use raises questions about consent, proportionality, access to collected data and the consequences of incorrect identification.

Identity, information and trust

AI-generated audio, images and video can imitate real people. Such manipulated or synthetic media are often called deepfakes. They may be used to impersonate a colleague, relative or public figure and make a false request appear authentic.

For example, a voice message that sounds like a family member might urgently request money. Recognising the voice alone is not enough to confirm the request. Contacting the person through a previously known number provides a separate way to check what happened.

AI can also produce misleading articles, fabricated images and large numbers of social media posts. Repeated exposure can make an unsupported claim seem widely accepted. Evaluating the original source, context and independent evidence is more reliable than judging whether content looks polished.

Not all AI-generated content is deceptive. The risk arises when synthetic content is presented in a misleading way or used to impersonate someone without their consent.

Vulnerabilities in AI systems

AI systems can themselves become targets of attacks. Their behaviour depends on training data, incoming instructions, external information and the tools they are allowed to use.

Prompt injection

Prompt injection occurs when malicious instructions in input or external content attempt to redirect an AI system away from its intended task. For example, a document being summarised could contain instructions telling the system to ignore the user's request or reveal information.

The potential impact is greater when the AI system can access private documents or perform actions. Separating untrusted content from instructions and restricting tool permissions can help limit the consequences of manipulation.

Data poisoning and backdoors

Data poisoning involves deliberately modifying data used to train or update a model in order to influence its behaviour. A backdoor is a hidden behaviour that may be activated by a particular trigger. Such attacks can undermine reliability even when the system appears to behave normally in other situations.

Reliability and human oversight

AI-generated answers can be fluent and confident while still being incorrect. A system may invent facts, references or explanations. These outputs are often described as hallucinations. Confidence in the wording should not be confused with evidence of accuracy.

In cybersecurity, incorrect advice could lead someone to overlook a threat or apply an unsafe configuration. Important conclusions should be checked against reliable documentation and appropriate testing.

Bias and unfair decisions

An AI system may perform differently across groups because of its training data, design or deployment conditions. A security system could incorrectly flag legitimate activity as suspicious or fail to recognise certain users accurately.

Human oversight should include reviewing significant decisions, investigating mistakes and providing a way for affected users to challenge incorrect outcomes. Simply placing a person near an automated process does not guarantee meaningful review.

Reducing the risks

Managing AI risks requires both responsible user behaviour and careful system design. The safeguards should reflect the sensitivity of the information and the possible consequences of an error or misuse.

  • Limit sensitive inputs: avoid submitting passwords, confidential documents or unnecessary personal information.
  • Verify unusual requests: confirm requests for money, credentials or sensitive actions through an independent, trusted channel.
  • Check important outputs: verify factual claims and review generated code before relying on it.
  • Restrict permissions: give AI systems only the data and tools needed for their intended tasks.
  • Review consequential actions: require meaningful human approval before actions with significant security or privacy consequences.
  • Monitor and improve: investigate errors, suspicious behaviour and unexpected outputs, then adjust safeguards.

No single measure removes every AI-related risk. Combining data protection, access controls, verification and human judgement helps reduce both deliberate misuse and accidental harm.
