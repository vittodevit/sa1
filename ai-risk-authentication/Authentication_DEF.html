What is authentication?

Authentication is the process of checking whether a person or system is who they claim to be. On a website, this usually means verifying that someone controls the credentials associated with an account. Simply knowing an email address or username is not enough: the person must provide evidence that the service accepts.

We use authentication when we open a university portal, access email or sign in to an online shop. Without it, private account information could be available to anyone who claimed to be the account owner. A successful check gives the service a basis for trusting the login, although no authentication method removes every possible risk.

Authentication does not always establish a person's legal identity. A discussion forum may verify control of an account with a nickname without knowing the user's real name. This distinction matters because protecting an account does not necessarily require collecting identity documents or other sensitive information.

Identification, authentication and authorisation

These terms describe different decisions. Identification tells the system which account is being claimed. Authentication checks the evidence for that claim. Authorisation determines which resources and actions are allowed.

A university portal example

  • Identification: a student enters their university email address.
  • Authentication: the portal verifies the student's login credentials.
  • Authorisation: the portal allows the student to view their own results, but does not let them change another student's grades.

A valid login therefore does not grant unlimited access. The service must still check permissions whenever a protected resource is requested. Public material, such as a university's course catalogue, may be accessible without any login.

What can prove control of an account?

Authentication methods are commonly grouped according to the kind of evidence they use. Each category has different strengths and limitations.

Something you know

A password, passphrase or PIN is a secret that the user knows. Such secrets are convenient, but they may be guessed, reused across services or disclosed through a scam. Two passwords still belong to the same category; they do not become two different authentication factors.

Something you have

A security key or a device holding an authentication credential can demonstrate possession. The system may ask the device to produce a response that someone without that credential cannot produce. Losing a device makes a secure recovery method important.

Something you are

Biometric methods use characteristics such as a fingerprint or face. They are convenient, but matching can make mistakes and biometric information requires careful protection. In many passkey systems, the fingerprint or face check happens on the device and unlocks a cryptographic credential; the website does not receive the fingerprint or face scan.

Combining different factor types is called multi-factor authentication. This is a basic concept within the topic, while the subtopics below explore particular technologies and security challenges.

What happens during a login?

The exact sequence depends on the method. A typical password-based website follows this simplified process:

  1. Account selection: the user supplies a username or email address.
  2. Credential submission: the browser sends the password through an encrypted HTTPS connection.
  3. Verification: the server checks the supplied password against a securely stored password hash, rather than retrieving a readable copy of the password.
  4. Additional checks: the service may request further evidence before accepting the login.
  5. Session creation: the service establishes an authenticated session, often represented in the browser by a cookie containing a session identifier.

A session lets the user move between pages without entering the password on every request. The session identifier is sensitive: someone who steals it may be able to use the account without knowing the password. Expiration and server-side invalidation at logout help limit this exposure.

How adaptive authentication fits in

A service might request an extra check when an account is accessed from an unfamiliar device or when a sensitive action is attempted. Context helps estimate risk; a familiar location or device is not, by itself, proof of identity. Adaptive authentication concerns when checks change, whereas an authentication factor describes the evidence used.

Why authentication can fail

Attackers can target people as well as software. A fake login page may persuade a user to disclose credentials. Automated attempts may guess passwords or try username-password pairs exposed by another service. Reusing one password can therefore allow a breach elsewhere to affect several accounts.

Weak recovery procedures create another route into an account. If an attacker controls the recovery email address, a strong password on the original account may not be enough. Security must cover enrolment, everyday access, recovery and the removal of lost or stolen authenticators.

Protection requires several complementary measures: unique passwords, careful handling of login requests, secure recovery options and appropriate authentication methods. Website operators also need to limit automated attempts, protect credentials and maintain secure sessions. A well-designed login screen alone cannot provide these protections.

Authentication and digital privacy

Authentication supports privacy by restricting access to personal information. However, the authentication process can also collect information about users. Device characteristics, login locations and biometric data may themselves be sensitive.

A service should consider which information it actually needs, how long it keeps it and who can access it. Users should be able to understand why information is requested. For example, tracking a detailed history of movements would raise different privacy concerns from recording that an unfamiliar device attempted to log in.

Security also needs to be usable. People may lose a phone, have difficulty using a biometric sensor or lack access to a particular device. Suitable alternative methods and carefully designed recovery procedures help legitimate users regain access without making impersonation easy.
