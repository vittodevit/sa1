Adaptive Authentication

Not every login deserves the same level of suspicion. Adaptive authentication, also called risk-based authentication, looks at the context of each attempt and asks for extra proof only when something looks unusual.

From static rules to risk-based decisions

Traditional login flows treat every attempt the same way: type your password, then confirm a one-time code, every single time. This is predictable for users and attackers alike. Adaptive authentication changes the question from "what is your password?" to "does this login look like you?".

Instead of always applying the strongest check, the system evaluates signals about the request, compares them with what it knows about the account, and calculates a risk score. The higher the score, the stronger the challenge.

Step 01

Collect context

Device, browser, network, location and the time of the request are gathered silently, before the user types anything.

Step 02

Score the risk

A model compares those signals with the account's normal behaviour and with threat intelligence about stolen credentials or malicious IPs.

Step 03

Step up if needed

A familiar laptop may log in with a passkey. A new device in another country triggers a code, a biometric check or a block.

What the risk engine looks at

No single signal proves who is logging in. The strength of adaptive authentication comes from combining several weak signals into one decision.

Common risk signals and how they are interpreted
Signal Examples Typical response
Device Known laptop, new phone, browser fingerprint, missing security patch Extra check
Network and location Home Wi-Fi, corporate VPN, foreign IP, impossible travel between two countries Step-up or block
Time and behaviour Login at 3 a.m., unusual typing rhythm, mouse movement, navigation pattern Extra check
Threat intelligence Credentials found in a breach, IP on a botnet list, request from a known attack tool Step-up or block
Established session Trusted device, recent successful verification, consistent activity Allow

Signals are combined, not judged individually: a new device alone may only require a code, while a new device in a new country at an unusual hour should require much more.

Three levels of friction

Risk level and the authentication challenge
Risk level What the system sees Challenge
Low Trusted device, usual location and normal working hours Password or passkey. No interruption.
Medium New device or an unusual time, but no other warning signs A push notification or a one-time code.
High Impossible travel, leaked credentials or automated attack patterns Phishing-resistant factor, manual verification or an outright block.

Why the weakest factor is never enough

SMS codes can be intercepted and push notifications can be approved by mistake. When the risk is high, the challenge should use a factor that cannot be phished, such as a hardware security key or a passkey bound to the device.

Where AI fits in

Rule-based systems can compare a device to a list, but they struggle with the long tail of "normal". Machine learning models learn each user's routine, including behavioural biometrics such as typing speed, swipe gestures and the way someone navigates a page.

This makes it possible to detect a stolen session even after a correct password: the attacker may have the credentials, but not the victim's habits. The model continuously re-scores the session instead of trusting it forever after the first login.

When the model gets it wrong

A false positive locks out a legitimate user, often at the worst possible moment. A false negative lets an attacker in with a normal-looking session. Models can also drift as devices and habits change, and they can inherit bias if the training data under-represents some groups of users. Adaptive systems therefore need monitoring, fallback paths and human support, not just a score.

Behavioural biometrics and location data are personal data. Under the GDPR they need a lawful basis, clear notice and a limited retention period. A system that scores users in secret, without an explanation or an appeal process, trades one security problem for another.

A login in practice

  1. 09:02 · Known device

    Low risk

    The user opens the banking app on the phone used every day, on the home Wi-Fi network. The passkey unlocks the session without any extra prompt.

  2. 09:05 · New context

    Score rises

    A second login arrives from a laptop never seen before, in a different country, ten minutes later. The combination is physically impossible, so the risk score jumps to high.

  3. 09:06 · Step-up challenge

    Extra proof required

    The session is paused and the user is asked to confirm with a hardware key or a biometric check on the trusted phone. A one-time code alone would not be enough at this level.

  4. 09:07 · Continuous monitoring

    Trust is earned again

    After verification, the new device is added to the trusted list but the session keeps being scored. A sudden change in behaviour would trigger another check.

Why it matters

Adaptive authentication does not replace multi-factor authentication; it decides when MFA is necessary and how strong it must be. Used well, it keeps accounts safer without forcing users through the same heavy flow at every login.

99.9% Of account compromise attacks are blocked by MFA
90% Of targeted attacks are stopped by on-device prompts
81% Of hacking breaches involve weak or stolen passwords

Sources: Microsoft, Google Security Blog and Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report.

Best practices

Building a sound adaptive flow

  • Prefer phishing-resistant factors: passkeys and hardware keys should be the default for high-risk decisions, not SMS codes.
  • Combine independent signals: never let a single device or IP decide the outcome on its own.
  • Explain the challenge: tell users why extra verification is requested, so the prompt does not look like a scam.
  • Always keep a fallback: account recovery must work when a device is lost, a number changes or a model is wrong.
  • Monitor fairness and drift: review false positives and error rates across user groups, and retrain the model when behaviour changes.
  • Log and limit data: collect only what the decision needs, and delete it when it is no longer useful.

Frequently asked questions

Is adaptive authentication the same as MFA?

No. MFA is the set of factors used to prove identity. Adaptive authentication is the decision layer that chooses which factors to request, and when, based on the risk of the attempt.

Does behavioural biometrics record what I type?

Well-designed systems do not store the content of what you type. They measure rhythm and patterns, such as typing speed or mouse movement, and keep only the features needed to compare your behaviour with your usual routine.

Useful resources

For the technical requirements behind authenticators and risk-based flows, see NIST SP 800-63B. For practical guidance on turning on MFA, visit CISA, and for passkey standards consult the FIDO Alliance.

Not a replacement for security habits

Adaptive authentication reduces friction, but it cannot compensate for reused passwords, missing updates or a user who approves every prompt without reading it.
