Authentication

Phishing and Fake Login Pages

Most stolen accounts are not hacked in any technical sense: the owner simply types the password into the wrong page. A good fake login page looks exactly like the real one, and that is all an attacker needs.

How a fake login page works

  1. Step 1

    The bait

    It usually begins with a message from the post office, your bank or the university IT service. Your parcel is stuck, your account will be locked tomorrow, something needs your attention right now.

  2. Step 2

    The link

    There is always a link or a QR code to fix the problem. The address looks believable if you only glance at it, but the site behind it belongs to the attacker.

  3. Step 3

    The cloned page

    Copying a login page takes minutes, so the logo, the colors and the layout are all there. Even the padlock is there, because getting an HTTPS certificate is free.

  4. Step 4

    The stolen credentials

    Whatever the victim types goes straight to the attacker, who often logs in within minutes. The more advanced phishing kits even forward the two-factor code while it is still valid.

Real or fake?

Clues that reveal a fake login page
ClueReal pageFake page
Address barThe exact domain of the service, for example usi.chA similar name with extra words, like usi-login-secure.com
Padlock (HTTPS)PresentUsually there too, since it only means the connection is encrypted
Spelling and designCorrectSmall mistakes, a slightly blurry logo
Tone of the messageCalmUrgent: "act now", "your account will be closed"

Common techniques

Lookalike domains

One character is swapped, as in "paypa1.com", or the real brand is buried in a longer name. Read quickly, and your brain fills in what it expects to see.

Browser-in-the-Browser

The page draws its own "Sign in with Google" window, complete with a fake address bar. It is just part of the page, which is why checking the URL inside it proves nothing.

QR code phishing

A sticker on a parking meter or a code in an email hides the link completely. You only see where it goes once you are already there.

How to protect yourself

  • Do not log in from a link: if a message asks you to sign in, open the site yourself from a bookmark instead.
  • Read the domain: what matters is the name right before .com or .ch, everything on the left of it can be faked.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication: a stolen password alone will not get anyone in.
  • Use passkeys: a passkey only works on the real website, so a copy has nothing to steal.

A password manager is also a good warning sign: if it does not offer to fill in your password, you are probably not on the site you think. See the password managers page.
