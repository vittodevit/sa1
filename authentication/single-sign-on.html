Single Sign-On and Social Login

Many websites let you sign in using an account you already have, such as Google, Apple, or Facebook. This is convenient because you don’t need to create another password. But if that account is compromised, it could give someone access to other services you use.

What is single sign-on?

Single sign-on means logging in once through a trusted service to access several other services. Social login is a familiar example, you use a button such as "Sign in with Google" to sign in to another website.

Example: SWITCH edu-ID at USI

At USI, a SWITCH edu-ID account gives access to services including iCorsi and the library. These services use the same university identity, so you don’t need a separate account for each one.

How "Sign in with Google" works

Here's what happens when you choose the button:

  1. Step 1

    You click "Sign in with Google"

  2. Step 2

    The website sends you to Google to log in.

  3. Step 3

    Google asks you if you want to share details like your name and email.

  4. Step 4

    Google confirms your identity, and the website logs you in without seeing your Google password.

Advantages and risks

Single sign-on is convenient, but relying on one account also creates risks.

Advantages and risks of single sign-on
Advantages Risks
Fewer passwords to remember. If your main account is stolen, attackers may access connected sites.
The website never sees your main account password. If the identity provider is unavailable, you may not be able to log in.
Two-factor authentication on your main account can help protect connected sites. The identity provider may see which services you use.
Signing up is faster. A website may request more personal data than it needs.

What data do you share?

When you use social login, a screen shows what information the website wants to receive. Many people click "Allow" without checking what they're sharing.

A website may ask to access information such as:

  • Your name
  • Your email address
  • Your profile picture
  • Your birthday or friends list

Real example: Facebook (2018)

In 2018, attackers exploited a Facebook bug to steal access tokens, digital keys that keep people logged in, from about 30 million accounts. Facebook's first estimate was nearly 50 million. Although Facebook said it found no evidence that attackers accessed third-party apps, the incident shows why a problem with one login provider could put connected services at risk.

Use social login safely

  • Turn on two-factor authentication for your Google, Apple or Facebook account.
  • Read the permission screen and allow only the information the app needs.
  • Review connected apps in your account settings and remove the ones you no longer use.
