Biometric Authentication

Fingerprints, faces and irises are unique enough to tell people apart, but turning a body characteristic into a login requires more than a camera or a sensor. Reliability, spoofing and privacy decide whether a biometric system is safe to use.

Identity factors By Giulio Finocchiaro

Something you are, not something you know

Passwords and tokens are something you know or something you have. Biometrics are something you are: a physical or behavioural trait that is measured, converted into a template and compared with a stored reference.

This makes biometrics convenient. There is nothing to remember and nothing to carry. It also makes them different from every other factor: a password can be changed after a breach, a fingerprint cannot. That single property drives most of the security and privacy questions around biometric authentication.

What can be measured

A biometric system is built around one or more modalities. Each has a different sensor, a different error profile and a different privacy cost.

Common biometric modalities
Modality What is measured Strengths Weaknesses
Fingerprint Ridges and minutiae on a fingertip Small sensors, fast, well understood Latent prints left on surfaces; difficult for manual workers
Face Facial geometry and texture Works at a distance, no contact Changes with age, lighting and pose; deepfake attacks
Iris Pattern of the iris Very high accuracy, hard to imitate Needs cooperation and a good camera; expensive
Voice Vocal tract and speaking style Works over the phone Affected by illness and noise; synthetic voice attacks
Behaviour Typing rhythm, gait, gestures Continuous and invisible Changes over time; weaker on its own

How matching works

A biometric system never compares two images directly. It extracts a template, a compact mathematical representation of the relevant features, and compares templates with a matching algorithm.

  1. Step 01 · Enrolment

    The reference is created

    The sensor captures one or more samples, quality checks are applied and a reference template is stored. Poor samples should be rejected here, not at login.

  2. Step 02 · Capture

    A new sample arrives

    At login the sensor captures a fresh sample. Liveness detection checks that it comes from a real person and not from a photo, a mask or a replay.

  3. Step 03 · Score

    Templates are compared

    The algorithm produces a similarity score. It is not a yes or no answer: it is a number that must be interpreted against a threshold.

  4. Step 04 · Decision

    The threshold decides

    A low threshold accepts more people but also more impostors. A high threshold does the opposite. Every deployment chooses where to sit on that trade-off.

1:1 and 1:N are not the same problem

Verification asks "is this person who they claim to be?" and compares one template with one reference. Identification asks "who is this person?" and searches a whole database. The second is much harder and has much higher privacy stakes.

Reliability and spoofing

Two error rates matter. The false acceptance rate (FAR) measures impostors wrongly accepted; the false rejection rate (FRR) measures legitimate users wrongly rejected. Lowering one usually raises the other.

Accuracy is not evenly distributed

Error rates change with age, skin tone, gender, lighting, camera quality and pose. Studies by NIST have repeatedly found that some face recognition algorithms perform worse for particular demographic groups. A system that works well on average can still fail systematically for some users.

A second problem is presentation attacks: a printed photo, a silicone finger, a replay video or a synthetic face. Good systems add liveness detection, depth sensing or challenge-response steps, but each addition costs money and can exclude users.

Privacy and law

Biometric data is sensitive by nature. Unlike a password, it cannot be reissued, and it often reveals more than the authentication decision needs. A fingerprint template is not a fingerprint, but it is still uniquely linked to a person and must be protected as personal data.

Under the GDPR, biometric data used to uniquely identify someone is a special category of personal data. Processing it generally requires explicit consent or another specific legal basis, plus a data protection impact assessment. The same logic appears in the EU AI Act and in several national laws.

Templates are not anonymous

Hashing or encrypting a template is not enough if the system can still compare it with new samples. Central databases also create a single target: one breach can expose a trait that users cannot change. On-device matching avoids sending raw biometrics to a server and is often the safer design.

There is also function creep: data collected for one purpose, such as unlocking a phone, can later be reused for attendance tracking, payments or surveillance. Clear limits on purpose, retention and access are part of the security of the system, not an afterthought.

Best practices

Designing a biometric flow that deserves trust

  • Keep biometrics on the device: match locally and send only a signed result or a derived key, never the raw template.
  • Test liveness, not just similarity: presentation attack detection should be evaluated against photos, masks, replays and synthetic media.
  • Offer a fallback: users with injuries, disabilities or changed appearance need another way in.
  • Measure error rates by group: report FAR and FRR across demographics, not only on the average user.
  • Minimise and delete: collect the minimum quality needed and set a retention period for every template.
  • Be transparent: tell users what is captured, where it is stored and how they can withdraw consent.

Frequently asked questions

Can a stolen fingerprint be used to log in?

A raw fingerprint image is not enough if the system checks liveness. A copied or synthetic finger can still succeed against weak sensors, which is why presentation attack detection matters more than the fingerprint itself.

Is face recognition on my phone the same as police face recognition?

No. Unlocking a phone is usually a 1:1 match performed on the device, with the template stored locally. Police systems often perform 1:N searches against large databases, with very different accuracy, legal and privacy implications.

  • EDPB, Guidelines 05/2022 on face recognition technology

Biometrics are identifiers, not secrets

A fingerprint can be photographed, a face can be reconstructed and a voice can be cloned. Biometrics should be paired with another factor, protected by liveness checks and never treated as something that can be reset after a leak.
