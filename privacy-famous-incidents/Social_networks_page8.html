Cybersecurity & Privacy

Social networks

What social networks are

A social network is an online platform that lets people create a profile, publish content, connect with other users, and interact through messages, comments, reactions, and sharing.

Well-known examples include Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, X, Snapchat, LinkedIn, YouTube, WhatsApp, Telegram, and Reddit.

From a privacy perspective they are special because the users themselves produce most of the content, and that content is personal by nature: faces, opinions, relationships, places, habits, and moods.

The four sections below follow the life cycle of personal information: how it circulated before social networks, how these platforms changed it, how they collect and use it, and what consequences this can have in the long run.

How was personal information shared before social networking platforms became widespread?

Before social networks, personal information circulated mainly in limited, local, and mostly temporary ways. The audience was usually small and known by the person sharing, and information tended to fade with time.

Offline sharing

  • Face-to-face conversation:
    information stayed within the people present and left no permanent record.
  • Letters, postcards, and telephone calls:
    communication was directed at one recipient or a small group, and the content was protected by postal and telecommunications secrecy.
  • Printed photographs and albums:
    pictures were physical objects, shown to visitors, relatives, and friends who were physically present.
  • Telephone directories and address books:
    name, address, and number were published, but they said little else about a person.
  • Local community channels:
    school yearbooks, club newsletters, notice boards, and local newspapers reached a bounded community.
  • Word of mouth and gossip:
    information could spread, but slowly, imprecisely, and with no searchable archive.

Early online sharing (before modern social networks)

The early internet already allowed people to share information, but in a much more fragmented way.

  • Email, mailing lists, and Usenet newsgroups.
  • Internet Relay Chat (IRC) and instant messengers such as ICQ and MSN Messenger.
  • Forums and bulletin board systems, often under pseudonyms.
  • Personal homepages and early blogs, created by people with some technical skills.
  • Early community sites such as GeoCities, and later the first social platforms such as Friendster and MySpace.

Characteristics of the pre-social-network era

Feature Before social networks
Audience Small, known, and chosen by the person sharing
Amount of information Limited and scattered across different places
Persistence Often temporary; memories and paper fade
Searchability Very low; finding information required effort
Control High; the person decided who saw a letter or an album
Identity Often pseudonymous online, separate from real life
Third-party collectors Mostly governments, banks, employers, and direct-marketing companies

In short, privacy was protected largely by practical obscurity: information was not secret, but it was so scattered and hard to gather that few people had the effort or the means to do it.

Of course, privacy was not perfect in this period. Institutions already kept files on individuals, and marketing companies traded mailing lists. What changed with social networks is the scale, speed, and permanence of the information.

Legal protection in that period

Even without social networks, the law already protected private life. These principles later became the basis for modern data protection rules.

  • Secrecy of correspondence and telecommunications:
    letters and calls could not be intercepted or opened without a legal basis.
  • Personality rights:
    in Switzerland, Article 28 of the Civil Code protects a person's personality, including the right to one's own image and to private life, against unlawful violations.
  • Professional secrecy:
    doctors, lawyers, and banks were bound by confidentiality duties.
  • First data protection laws:
    from the 1970s onward, countries started regulating the files kept by public bodies and companies, because computers made large databases possible.

A short timeline: from the first online communities to social networks

Period Development Effect on personal information
1980s - 1990s Email, bulletin boards, Usenet, IRC Communication between small, interest-based groups, often pseudonymous
Late 1990s Personal homepages, early blogs, instant messengers People begin to publish information about themselves, but with limited reach
2002 - 2003 Friendster, LinkedIn, MySpace First profiles with lists of friends and connections
2004 - 2006 Facebook, YouTube, Twitter Real identities, photo sharing, and the first feeds
2007 - 2010 Smartphones, mobile apps, Instagram Constant sharing, photos with location, always-on connection
2011 - 2018 Snapchat, algorithmic feeds, TikTok's international growth Recommendation algorithms and behavioral advertising become central
2018 onward GDPR, data scandals, generative AI Stronger regulation, but also new uses of user content

This timeline shows a progressive shift: from information that people sent to someone to information that people publish on a platform, where a company stores it and manages its circulation.

How did social networks change the amount, accessibility, and visibility of personal information online?

Social networks transformed personal information in three main dimensions: there is much more of it, it is much easier to reach, and it is far more visible to people we do not know.

1. Amount: from occasional sharing to continuous sharing

Sharing used to be an occasional event, such as showing holiday pictures once. On social networks it became a continuous habit, often several times a day.

  • Photos and videos of daily life, including friends, family members, and children.
  • Opinions, jokes, political views, and reactions to the news.
  • Real-time location through tagged places, check-ins, and stories.
  • Relationships: who our friends, partners, colleagues, and relatives are.
  • Education, employment history, interests, and life events.
  • Implicit information: what we like, what we watch until the end, and what we search for.

Smartphones strengthened this trend because a camera, a GPS, and a connection are always in the pocket.

Information is also created about us by others: a friend can tag us in a photo, mention us in a post, or upload a contact list that includes our number, even if we never opened an account.

2. Accessibility: from scattered to centralized and searchable

  • Centralization:
    data that used to be spread over albums, letters, and directories now sits in one profile.
  • Search:
    a name, a username, a photo, or a phone number is often enough to find a person and their activity.
  • Permanence:
    posts, comments, and photos remain stored unless deleted, and they may be copied by other users or archived by third parties.
  • Replication:
    a screenshot or a repost means that deleting the original does not always remove all the copies.
  • Aggregation:
    pieces of information that look harmless can be combined to build a detailed picture of someone's life, routines, and weaknesses.
  • Technical access:
    public profiles can be collected automatically (scraping), and some platforms offer interfaces (APIs) that let third-party applications retrieve data.

3. Visibility: from a chosen circle to an unknown audience

Before, people decided who would see a letter or an album. On social networks the real audience is often larger and less predictable than the person imagines.

  • Public-by-default settings or confusing privacy controls can expose content to everyone.
  • Context collapse: family, friends, colleagues, and strangers are mixed in one audience, so a post meant for one group may be read by another.
  • Friends of friends, group members, and followers can see or share content beyond the original circle.
  • Algorithms decide what is amplified: content can reach people far outside the intended audience.
  • Employers, universities, insurers, journalists, and criminals can look at profiles.

Comparison

Dimension Before social networks With social networks
Amount Occasional and limited Continuous and detailed
Accessibility Scattered and hard to search Centralized, indexed, and searchable
Visibility Small, known audience Potentially global and unknown audience
Duration Often temporary Often permanent and copyable
Who produces the data Mostly the person The person, friends, family, and the platform itself

Important: social networks also have real benefits, such as staying in touch, community building, access to information, and professional opportunities. The privacy problem is not the existence of sharing, but the loss of control over how far the information travels.

The illusion of control

Social networks give users many tools: audience selection, blocking, hidden lists, disappearing stories. These tools are useful, but they protect mainly against other users, not against the platform itself, which can still see and analyze everything.

  • Settings are often complex, change over time, and are located in different menus.
  • Default options frequently favor sharing and data collection.
  • A "private" account can still be exposed if a follower takes a screenshot or shares a message.
  • Disappearing content is not necessarily gone: it can be saved by recipients and may remain on the platform's servers for a period.

Shadow profiles and information about non-users

A person does not need an account to appear in a social network's data. When a user uploads the phone's address book, the platform receives names, numbers, and emails of people who may never have joined. Photos and tags can also identify non-members. Platforms can use this information to suggest contacts or to build incomplete profiles, sometimes called shadow profiles.

Network effects and the cost of leaving

The value of a social network depends on how many people use it. If friends, classmates, or colleagues are all on a platform, leaving it has a social cost. This weakens the user's bargaining power: it becomes difficult to refuse privacy-invasive terms when the alternative is isolation.

Data portability rules (such as Article 20 of the GDPR) aim to reduce this lock-in by allowing users to obtain their data in a structured format, but moving the social connections themselves remains difficult.

Open-source intelligence (OSINT) and everyday search

Publicly available information on social networks is used by many different actors, not always with bad intentions.

  • Employers and recruiters who check candidates.
  • Journalists and researchers who verify identities and events.
  • Investigators, insurers, and authorities who gather public evidence.
  • Criminals who prepare scams or burglaries.
  • Acquaintances, ex-partners, and curious strangers.

The same public post can therefore be used legitimately by one person and abusively by another. The user cannot predict who will look, or when.

How do social media platforms collect and use information about their users?

Most social networks are free for users because their business model is based on advertising. The platform offers a free service, collects data about users, and uses it to sell highly targeted advertising space. In other words, personal data is the raw material of the business.

What information is collected?

Category Examples
Information given directly Name, email, phone number, date of birth, profile picture, biography, workplace, school
Content created Posts, photos, videos, comments, messages, voice recordings, stories
Behavioral data Likes, shares, searches, time spent on each post, videos watched, clicks, scrolling patterns
Social graph Friends, followers, groups, contacts imported from the phone, interactions with each person
Device and technical data IP address, device model, operating system, browser, language, battery and network information, advertising identifiers
Location data GPS, Wi-Fi networks, IP-based location, photo metadata, tagged places
Data from other websites Like buttons, tracking pixels, and SDKs embedded in third-party sites and apps
Data from partners and brokers Purchase history, demographic information, and offline data bought from data brokers
Inferred data Interests, income level, political leaning, relationship status, health concerns, personality traits

The last category is particularly important: platforms do not only store what users say, they infer things users never stated. These inferences may concern sensitive information such as sexual orientation, religion, or health, even if the user never published it.

How is the information collected?

  • When the user fills in forms and uses the service.
  • Through apps that run on the phone and ask for permissions such as contacts, camera, microphone, and location.
  • Through cookies, pixels, and tracking tools on websites that have nothing to do with the platform.
  • Through facial recognition or automatic analysis of photos and videos, where permitted.
  • Through other users who upload contacts, tag people, or mention them.
  • Through purchase of data from third parties.

How is the information used?

  1. Targeted advertising
    Advertisers choose an audience by age, location, interests, and behavior, and the platform shows the advertisement to matching users. This is the main source of revenue.
  2. Personalization and recommendation algorithms
    The feed, suggested friends, recommended videos, and notifications are selected according to the user's profile, usually to maximize time spent and engagement.
  3. Profiling
    Behavioral data is combined into a detailed profile that predicts what a user might like, buy, or believe.
  4. Analytics and product improvement
    Usage statistics are used to test new features and measure what keeps users active.
  5. Security and moderation
    Data helps to detect fake accounts, spam, fraud, abuse, and illegal content.
  6. Training of AI systems
    Public posts, images, and interactions may be used to train recommendation, moderation, or generative AI models, depending on platform policies and applicable law.
  7. Sharing with third parties
    Data can be shared with advertisers (often in aggregated form), service providers, business partners, researchers, and authorities when legally required.

Consent, transparency, and legal limits

Platforms usually rely on terms of service and privacy policies, which users accept when they register. In practice these documents are long, technical, and rarely read, so consent is often more formal than informed.

Privacy laws such as the GDPR and the Swiss FADP require a lawful basis, transparency, purpose limitation, and data minimization, and they give users rights such as access, correction, deletion, and objection to profiling and direct marketing.

Platforms may also offer privacy settings, data download tools, and ad preference controls, but the default settings often favor data collection, and users must actively change them.

A key point: "free" does not mean without cost. The user pays with attention and personal data.

Tracking technologies used by platforms

Technology How it works Privacy effect
Cookies Small files stored in the browser that recognize a returning user Follow activity across sessions and, if third-party, across sites
Tracking pixels Tiny invisible images or scripts embedded in websites and emails Report visits, clicks, and purchases back to the platform
SDKs in mobile apps Code from the platform integrated into other apps Share app usage and device identifiers with the platform
Advertising identifiers Unique code assigned to a phone Link activity in different apps to the same device
Device fingerprinting Combination of browser, fonts, screen, and system characteristics Identifies a device even when cookies are deleted
Metadata Hidden data attached to files and messages, such as time and location of a photo Reveals where, when, and with what device content was created

How an advertisement reaches you: a simplified path

  1. The user's activity on and off the platform is collected and linked to a profile or identifier.
  2. The platform assigns the user to categories (for example "interested in running" or "recently moved").
  3. An advertiser chooses a target audience and a budget.
  4. When the user opens the app, an automated auction decides which advertisement is shown.
  5. The platform measures whether the user viewed, clicked, or bought, and feeds this result back into the profile.

This cycle repeats millions of times per second across the platform, so the profile becomes more precise over time.

Data brokers and the wider data economy

Data brokers are companies that collect personal information from many sources (public records, loyalty cards, apps, websites, platforms), combine it, and sell or license it. Social media data can be merged with these databases, which allows the creation of profiles that are much richer than anything the user knowingly shared on a single platform.

Notable cases

Case What happened Why it matters
Cambridge Analytica (2018) - Facebook Newsroom, 4 April 2018 Data of up to about 87 million Facebook users was obtained through a personality-quiz app and its friend connections, and used for political profiling Showed that data shared with a third-party app could spread far beyond the users who gave consent
Facebook and the US Federal Trade Commission (2019) - FTC press release A record 5 billion US dollar penalty and new compliance obligations for privacy violations Demonstrated that regulators can impose heavy sanctions on large platforms
Meta and the Irish regulator (2023) - EDPB announcement A fine of 1.2 billion euros for transferring European users' data to the United States without adequate safeguards Illustrates the importance of international data transfer rules
TikTok and the Irish regulator (2023) - Irish DPC press release A fine of 345 million euros concerning the processing of children's data and default settings Shows the focus of regulators on minors and privacy-by-default
Facebook data scraping (2022 decision) - Irish DPC press release After personal data of Facebook users was found published online in 2021, the DPC fined Meta 265 million euros for breaching data protection by design and by default Shows that publicly accessible data can be harvested automatically at large scale, and that platform design choices are a legal responsibility

Legal rules that apply to social networks

  • GDPR:
    applies to platforms offering services to people in the EU. It requires a legal basis, transparency, data minimization, and respect for user rights. Consent for targeted advertising must be freely given, specific, and informed.
  • Swiss FADP:
    applies to processing of personal data in Switzerland and to foreign effects on Switzerland. It requires transparency, proportionality, privacy by design and by default, and gives access and correction rights.
  • Digital Services Act (DSA):
    for large platforms, it restricts advertising based on profiling with sensitive data, prohibits profiling-based advertising aimed at minors, and requires more transparency about recommendation systems.
  • Children's rules:
    for example GDPR Article 8 and the US COPPA, which require parental authorization or stronger protections for minors.
  • Right to object:
    users can object to direct marketing and, in certain cases, to profiling.

These rules are not a guarantee that every platform behaves correctly, but they give users concrete rights, and they allow regulators and courts to intervene.

What long-term consequences can social media activity have for personal privacy?

The most important feature of social media privacy risks is that they are often delayed. A post that seems harmless today can have effects years later, because information is stored, copied, and reinterpreted in new contexts.

1. A permanent digital footprint

Even deleted content may survive in screenshots, archives, search engine caches, backups, or the memory of other users. Old posts, photos, and opinions from adolescence can resurface in adulthood.

2. Impact on education and employment

  • Recruiters and universities may look at public profiles when evaluating candidates.
  • Old comments, inappropriate photos, or controversial opinions can damage a reputation.
  • Content taken out of context may be judged by standards that did not exist when it was posted.

3. Identity theft, fraud, and social engineering

  • Name, birthday, hometown, school, pet's name, and relatives are details often used for security questions and password guessing.
  • Attackers can build convincing phishing or impersonation messages using personal details (spear phishing).
  • Cloned profiles, fake accounts, and deepfakes can use a person's photos and voice to scam friends and relatives.
  • Data leaked from a platform can be reused for years in other attacks.

4. Physical safety, stalking, and harassment

Real-time posts, tagged locations, and photos of a home, workplace, or school can reveal routines and addresses. This can facilitate stalking, burglary, doxxing, or harassment. Metadata in photos can also reveal where and when a picture was taken.

5. Profiling, manipulation, and discrimination

  • Detailed profiles can be used to influence purchasing decisions, political opinions, and emotions through personalized content.
  • Inferences about health, finances, or personality may indirectly influence offers, prices, or opportunities.
  • Algorithms can create filter bubbles and echo chambers that limit exposure to different viewpoints.

6. Data breaches, scraping, and data brokers

Large platforms are attractive targets for attackers. Leaked or scraped data can be published, sold, and combined with other databases, and it is practically impossible to withdraw once it is circulating.

7. Effects on other people

A person's activity also affects friends and family. Posting a photo of a child, tagging a friend, or sharing a group chat can expose people who did not consent. Children are especially affected, since parents may build a digital identity for them before they are able to decide for themselves ("sharenting").

8. Changes in platform ownership, rules, and laws

Data shared under today's policy can be used under tomorrow's. Platforms can be sold, change their terms, introduce new features such as AI training, or go bankrupt, and users' data may be transferred or reused in ways they did not anticipate.

9. Psychological and social effects

Constant sharing and comparison, public criticism, and online harassment can affect well-being and self-image. The permanence of content can make it harder to move on from past mistakes.

Summary of consequences

Risk Typical example Time horizon
Reputation damage An old post is found during a job application Years

How to reduce the risks

  1. Review privacy settings regularly and prefer restricted audiences over public sharing.
  2. Think before posting: would I be comfortable if this were seen by a future employer, a stranger, or my family?
  3. Avoid publishing sensitive details such as address, documents, tickets, and real-time location.
  4. Remove location metadata from photos and avoid posting live whereabouts.
  5. Limit app permissions (contacts, microphone, location) and the number of connected third-party apps.
  6. Use strong, unique passwords and multi-factor authentication, since compromised accounts expose private data.
  7. Ask for permission before posting photos of other people, especially children.
  8. Use the rights offered by law: request access to your data, correct it, delete it, or object to profiling.
  9. Periodically delete old posts, unused accounts, and unnecessary connections.
  10. Be critical of quizzes, giveaways, and links that ask for personal data.

The right to be forgotten and its limits

Under Article 17 of the GDPR, individuals can in certain circumstances ask for the erasure of their personal data. In the 2014 Google Spain judgment, the Court of Justice of the European Union also recognized that a person can ask search engines to remove certain links to outdated or irrelevant information about them.

However, this right has limits:

  • It must be balanced against freedom of expression and the public interest.
  • Removal from a platform does not guarantee removal of copies shared by other people.
  • Information about public figures and matters of public interest is more difficult to remove.
  • The process can take time, and the user must know where the information is stored.

For this reason, prevention (not publishing) remains more effective than correction (trying to delete later).

Wider consequences for society

  • Normalization of surveillance:
    people become used to being observed and measured, and may accept data collection as normal.
  • Chilling effect:
    awareness of being watched can lead people to avoid expressing opinions or joining certain groups.
  • Loss of anonymity:
    combining photos, names, and places makes it easier to identify individuals in public life.
  • Influence on democratic processes:
    detailed profiles allow micro-targeted political messages that are difficult for the public to monitor.
  • Unequal power:
    a few large companies hold enormous amounts of information, while individuals have little visibility over how it is used.

What to do if your privacy has been violated

  1. Collect evidence
    Take screenshots with date and URL of the content or message.
  2. Use the platform's reporting tools
    Report impersonation, harassment, non-consensual images, or privacy violations.
  3. Secure the account
    Change passwords, enable multi-factor authentication, and check connected devices and apps.
  4. Exercise your legal rights
    Send an access, deletion, or objection request to the platform's data protection contact.
  5. Contact the authority
    In Switzerland, the FDPIC; in the EU, the national data protection authority.
  6. Consider legal or police support
    For threats, fraud, extortion, or serious defamation, contact the police or a lawyer.

What you should remember

  1. Before: Personal information was shared in small, known circles, was scattered, and often disappeared with time.
  2. Change: Social networks increased the volume, searchability, permanence, and potential audience of personal information.
  3. Business model: Platforms collect, infer, and analyze data mainly to personalize content and sell targeted advertising.
  4. Consequences: Information shared today can affect reputation, security, and autonomy for many years, and it also affects other people.
  5. Control: Awareness, careful settings, and the exercise of legal rights are the most effective tools users have.

The central lesson is simple: whatever is posted online should be treated as potentially public, permanent, and reusable.
