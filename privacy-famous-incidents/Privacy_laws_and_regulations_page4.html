Cybersecurity & Privacy

Privacy laws and regulations

What privacy laws and regulations are

Privacy laws and regulations govern how governments, businesses, organizations, employers, websites, applications, and other entities collect, use, store, share, transfer, and delete personal information.

They determine what information can be collected, who can access it, what it can be used for, how long it can be retained, and what rights individuals have over their information.

Privacy law is one of the most important areas of modern law because almost every aspect of everyday life involves personal data: banking, healthcare, employment, education, shopping, social media, telecommunications, artificial intelligence, and government services.

Because Switzerland is a particularly relevant jurisdiction, I'll give special attention to Swiss privacy law and its relationship with European Union law.

Understanding privacy and data protection

First, it is important to distinguish several related concepts.

Concept Meaning
Privacy The right to maintain personal space, autonomy, and confidentiality.
Data protection Rules governing the processing of personal information.
Information security Measures that protect information against unauthorized access, alteration, loss, or destruction.
Confidentiality The obligation not to disclose protected information improperly.
Cybersecurity Protection of digital systems, networks, and information against cyber threats.
Data governance Policies and procedures governing how an organization manages information.

These contents overlap, but they are not identical.

For example, a company might have excellent cybersecurity but still violate privacy law by collecting unnecessary personal information or using it without an appropriate legal basis.

What is personal data?

Personal data is information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person.

Examples include:

  • Full name
  • Home address
  • Telephone number
  • Email address
  • Passport number
  • National identification number
  • Bank account details
  • Credit card information
  • IP addresses
  • Location information
  • Photographs and videos
  • Biometric information
  • Medical records
  • Employment records
  • Online identifiers
  • Browsing ad purchasing histories

Some information receives additional legal protection because it is particularly sensitive.

What is sensitive personal data?

Depending on the applicable law, sensitive information may include:

  • Racial or ethnic origin
  • Political opinions
  • Religious or philosophical beliefs
  • Trade union membership
  • Genetic information
  • Biometric information used for identification
  • Health information
  • Sexual orientation or sex-life information
  • Criminal convinctions and offences

The precise definition differs between jurisdictions.

important: Information does not necessarily stop being personal data simply because someone's name has been removed. If that person can still reasonably be identified, privacy legislation may continue to apply.

The fundamental principles of privacy laws

Most modern privacy frameworks are based on similar principles.

  1. Lawfulness, fairness and transparency
    Personal information must be processed legally, fairly, and in a transparent manner.
  2. Purpose limitation
    Information should be collected for specific, legitimate purposes and not used incompatibly with those purposes.
  3. Data minimization
    Organizations should collect only the information necessary for their purposes.
  4. Accuracy
    Personal information should be accurate and kept up to date where necessary.
  5. Storage limitation
    Personal information should not be retained longer than necessary.
  6. Integrity and confidentiality
    Organizations must protect personal information through appropriate security measures.
  7. Accountability
    Organizations must be able to demonstrate that they comply with applicable privacy requirements.

These principles are especially prominent in the European GDPR and influence many other privacy laws worldwide.

The world's most important privacy laws

There is no single privacy law that governs the entire world. Different countries and regions have developed their own legislation.

However, several major laws have become international benchmarks.

A. European Union - General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) (In force since 25 May 2018)

The GDPR is one of the most influential privacy laws in the world>

It protects personal data belonging to individuals in the European Economic Area and applies to organizations established in the EU, as well as certain organizations outside the EU that offer goods or services to people in the EU or monitor their behaviour there.

Who must comply?

  • EU-based companies.
  • International companies serving people in the EU.
  • Online platforms monitoring the behaviour of EU residents.
  • Employers processing employee information.
  • Healthcare providers and financial institutions.
  • Organizations handling personal data on behalf of other organizations.

The six legal bases for processing personal data

An organization generally needs at least one valid legal basis for processing personal data:

Legal basis Explanation
Consent The individual freely agrees to the processing.
Contract Pocessing is necessary to perform a contract or take requested pre-contractual steps.
Legal obligation Processing is required by law.
Vital interests Processing is necessary to protect someone's life or vital interest.
Public task Processing is necessary for a task carried out in the public interest or official authority.
Legitimate interests Processing is necessary for legitimate interests, subject to balancing against individuals' rights and freedoms.

Consent is therefore not the only legal basis for processing personal data.

Main rights under the GDPR

  1. Right to be informed.
  2. Right of access.
  3. Right to rectification.
  4. Right to erasure (the right to be forgotten).
  5. Right to restrict processing.
  6. Right to data portability.
  7. Right to object.
  8. Rights relating to certain automated decisions and profiling.

GDPR penalties (Maximum administrative fine)

  • Up to €10 million or 2% of the organization's worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.
  • Up to €20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

The higher level applies to more serious categories of infringement.

These are maximum limits, not automatic penalties. Authorities consider factors such as the nature, severity, duration, intent, and consequences of the violation.

Official resources: European Commission - GDPR legal framework.

B. Switzerland - Federal Act on Data Protection (FADP)(In force since 1 September 2023)

Switzerland has its own comprehensive privacy legislation. It is not an EU member, so the GDPR does not automatically apply to every Swiss organization.

The revised Swiss FADP modernized the country's data protection framework and brought it closer to the GDPR.

What does the Swiss FADP protect?

It protects the personality and fundamental rights of natural persons whose personal data is processed.

It applies to private organizations and federal public bodies, subject to its scope and statutory exceptions.

One important difference from the previous Swiss law is that the revised FADP generally protects natural persons, not legal entities such as corporations.

Main obligations under Swiss law

Organizations must, among the other things:

  • Process personal data lawfully and proportionately.
  • Provide appropriate transparency about data processing.
  • Protect information through appropriate technical and organizational measures.
  • Maintain records of processing activities when legally required.
  • Conduct data protection impact assessments when processing is likely to result in a high risk.
  • Notify the Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner (FDPIC) of certain data security breaches as soon as possible.
  • Comply with international data-transfer requirements.
  • Provide individuals with access to their personal data.

Individual rights under Swiss law

Individual generally have the right to:

  • Request information about their personal data.
  • Obtain information about the purposes of processing.
  • Learn about recipients or categories of recipients.
  • Learn about retention periods.
  • Request correction of inaccurate data.
  • Seek deletion or cessation of unlawful processing through applicable legal remedies.
  • Challenge certain decisions based solely on automated processing.

Swiss law also includes specific protections concerning automated individual decisions.

Criminal penalties

A significant difference between Swiss and EU law is the approach to sanctions.

Under the FADP, certain intentional violations of specified duties can result in criminal fines of up to CHF 250,000.

These fines generally target responsible natural persons, rather than automatically being calculated as a percentage of a company's global revenue.

Swiss data protection authority

Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner (FDPIC)

The FDPIC supervises federal bodies and private organizations within its jurisdiction, investigates potential violations, issues recommendations and can exercise statutory investigative powers.

Official legislation: Swiss Federal Act on Data Protection - Fedlex.

C. United States - Privacy laws

Unlike the EU, the United States does not have one comprehensive federal privacy law covering all personal information across all industries.

Instead, privacy protection is distributed across federal statutes, state laws, and regulations.

Important federal law

Law What it protects
HIPAA Protected health information held by covered healthcare entities and business associates.
GLBA Certain financial information held by financial institutions.
COPPA Online personal information collected from children under 13.
FCRA Consumer credit and background-reporting information.
Privacy Act of 1974 Personal records maintained by federal agencies.
VPPA Certain video-viewing information.

Important state privacy laws

The most prominent example is the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), as amended by the California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA).

Other states have developed their own frameworks, including:

  • Virginia Consumer Data Protection Act.
  • Colorado Privacy Act.
  • Connecticut Data Privacy Act.
  • Texas Data Privacy and Security Act.
  • Oregon Consumer Privacy Act.
  • Utah Consumer Privacy Act.
  • New Jersey Data Protection Act.

The requirements differ substantially between states.

California gives qualifying consumers rights to know, delete, correct, and opt out of certain sales or sharing of their personal information.

Official source: California Attorney General- CCPA.

Individual pivacy rights explained

Privacy laws are not only about controlling businesses. They also give individuals legal rights over their personal information.

Here is what the most important rights mean in practice.

1. Right to access

You can ask an organization:

  • Does it hold information about me?
  • What information does it have?
  • Where did it obtain the information?
  • Why is it processing the information?
  • Who has received my information?
  • How long will it retain my information?

Example: You ask a bank to provide a copy of the personal information it holds about you.

2. Right to rectification

You can request correction of inaccurate or incomplete personal information.

Example: Your employer has recorded the wrong residential address or date of birth.

3. Right to erasure

Sometimes called the right to be forgotten.

You can request deletion of personal information in circumstances specified by the applicable law.

For example:

  • The data is no longer necessary.
  • You withdraw consent and there is no other legal basis.
  • The organization has processed the information unlawfully.
  • A valid legal obligation requires deletion.

However, this right is not absolute.
Organizations may have legal reasons to retain information, such as tax, financial, regulatory, or legal recordkeeping requirements.

4. Right to restrict processing

You can sometimes require an organization to limit how it uses your information while a dispute or verification process takes place.

5. Rght to data portability

Under applicable laws, you may be entitled to receive certain personal information in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format.

You may also be able to transmit it to another service provider.

Example: Transferring certain personal information from one digital service to another.

6. Right to object

You can object to certain forms of personal data processing.

Under the GDPR, you have an unconditional right to object to processing for direct marketing purposes.

Other objections, such as those based on legitimate interests or public tasks, are subject to specific legal rules.

7. Rights concerning automated decision-making

This is becoming increasingly important because companies use artificial intelligence and algorithms to make decisions.

Examples include:

  • Automated credit scoring.
  • Recruitment screening.
  • Insurance risk assessments.
  • Fraud detection.
  • Automated eligibility decisions.
  • Customer profiling.

The GDPR provides protections against certain decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal or similarly significant effects, subject to exceptions and safeguards.

Swiss law also has notification and review protections concerning certain automated individual decisions.

Privacy laws concerning websites, cookies and online tracking

Websites and applications collect enormous amounts of personal information.

Privacy legislation regulates how that information is collected and used.

What are cookies?

Cookies are small pieces of information stored in a user's browser.

They can be used for:

  • Remembering login sessions.
  • Saving shopping carts.
  • Remembering language preferences.
  • Website analytics.
  • Advertising.
  • Tracking browsing activity.

Types of cookies

Type Purpose
Strictly necessary Essential website funcionality.
Functional Remember user preferences.
Analytical Measure website performance ad usage.
Advertising Deliver and measure advertisements.
Tracking Track activity across websites or services.

Are cookie legal?

Yes, but their use is subject to applicable laws.

In the EU, the ePrivacy Directive and national implementing laws regulate access to information stored on users' devices. Non-essential cookies generally require prior consent.

In Switzerland, the Telecommunications Act (TCA) contains rules on storing information on or accessing information from terminal equipment, including transparency and opt-out requirements. The FADP also applies where personal data is involved.

A cookie banner does not automatically make a website compliant. Its design, choices, consent mechanism, and actual tracking practices matter.

Website privacy policies

A website collecting personal data should provide appropriate privacy information.

A privacy notice commonly explains:

  • Who operates the website.
  • What personal information is collected.
  • Why information is collected.
  • The legal basis, where required.
  • Whether third parties receive information.
  • Whether data is transferred internationally.
  • How long information is retained.
  • What rights users have.
  • How users can contact the organization.
  • How they can submit privacy complaints.

A privacy policy is not permission to do anything with personal data.
The actual processing must still comply with the law.

Data breaches and cybersecurity regulations

A data breach occurs when personal information is accidentally or unlawfully:

  • Disclosed.
  • Accessed.
  • Lost.
  • Destroyed.
  • Altered.
  • Stolen.

Examples of data breaches

  • A hacker steals customer information.
  • An employee sends confidential files to the wrong recipient.
  • A company loses an unencrypted laptop.
  • A database is publicly accessible.
  • Ransomware encrypts customer records.
  • An employee accesses medical records without authorization.

What must organizations do?

The exact requirements depend on the applicable law.

Requirement GDPR Swiss FADP
Notify regulator Generally within 72 hours of awareness if the breach is likely to risk individuals' rights and freedoms As soon as possible if the breach is likely to result in a high risk to personality or fundamental rights
Notify affected individuals Without undue delay if the breach is likely to result in a high risk As appropriate, particularly when necessary for the protection of affected individuals
Document incidents Yes, where applicable Security and accountability obligations apply

A company should have a documented incident-response process.

Recommended breach-response procedure

  1. Detect the incident
    Identify suspicious activity, unauthorized access, or data loss.
  2. Contain the breach
    Restrict access, isolate compromised systems, and prevent further exposure.
  3. Investigate
    Establish what happened, which information was affected, and how many people are involved.
  4. Assess legal obligations
    Determine which regulators and affected individuals must be notified and by when.
  5. Notify relevant parties
    Provide accurate information and practical protective advice where required.
  6. Remediate
    Fix vulnerabilities, restore systems, and improve security.
  7. Document and review
    Record the incident and identify measures to prevent recurrence.

International data transfer

This is one of the most complicated areas of international privacy law.

Imagine a Swiss company collecting customer information in Zurich but storing it on servers in the United States.

Or a German company using an Indian company to process customer support requests.

Which country's privacy law applies?

Several laws may apply simultaneously.

What are international data transfers?

They involve transferring or making personal information accessible across national borders.

Examples include:

  • Cloud storage.
  • International outsourcing.
  • Global payroll services.
  • International customer databases.
  • Overseas call centers.
  • International marketing platforms.
  • Foreign IT support providers.

GDPR international transfers

Under the GDPR, transfers outside the European Economic Area generally require an approved transfer mechanism.

Common mechanisms include:

  1. Adequacy decisions
    The European Commission determines that a country provides an adequate level of data protection.
  2. Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs)
    Approved contractual clauses that impose data protection obligations on the parties.
  3. Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs)
    Internal rules governing international data transfers within multinational corporate groups.
  4. Specific derogations
    Certain limited exceptions may apply in particular circumstances.

Swiss international transfers

Under Article 16 of the Swiss FADP, personal data can generally be transferred to countries recognized as providing adequate protection.

Where adequate protection is absent, appropriate safeguards or a statutory exception are generally required.

The FDPIC recognizes certain standard contractual clauses.

An important example is the Swiss-US Data Privacy Framework: certified US organizations can receive personal data from Switzerland under the applicable adequacy arrangement.

Source: Swiss Federal Office of Justice – international data protection information

Privacy regulations in specific industries

Some industries have additional privacy requirements beyond general data protection legislation.

Healthcare organizations process highly sensitive personal information.

Typical protected information includes:

  • Diagnoses.
  • Medical histories.
  • Prescription records.
  • Laboratory results.
  • Medical imaging.
  • Genetic information
  • Treatment records.
  • Mental health information.

Relevant legislation includes:

  • GDPR and national healthcare confidentiality laws.
  • Swiss FADP and professional secrecy rules.
  • USA HIPAA.
  • Australia's My Health Records legislation and Privacy Act.
  • Canada's federal and provincial healthcare privacy legislation.

Banking and financial services

Financial institutions handle:

  • Account balances.
  • Transaction histories.
  • Credit records.
  • Identity documents.
  • Investment information.
  • Income information.
  • Payment information.

Relevant requirements may include:

  • GDPR.
  • Swiss FADP.
  • Swiss banking secrecy under Article 47 of the Banking Act.
  • US GLBA.
  • Financial sector cybersecurity and confidentiality requirements.
  • Anti-money laundering recordkeeping rules.

An important distinction: banking secrecy and data protection are related but separate legal concepts.

Employment and workplace privacy

Employers collect personal information about employees and applicants.

Examples:

  • CVs and applications.
  • Salary information.
  • Performance reviews.
  • Sick leave records.
  • Attendance records.
  • CCTV footage.
  • Email communications.
  • Computer usage.
  • Biometric attendance records.

Employers generally need a legitimate legal basis and must respect proportionality, transparency, and applicable employment laws.

In Switzerland, Article 328b of the Code of Obligations specifically limits employers' processing of employee data to information concerning the employee's suitability for employment or necessary for performing the employment contract, subject to the applicable statutory framework.

Education

Schools, universities, and educational platforms collect:

  • Student records.
  • Examination results.
  • Attendance records.
  • Disability-related information.
  • Parent and guardian details.
  • Learning-platform activity.

Additional child-protection and education-specific rules may apply.

Telecommunnications

Telecommunications companies process:

  • Telephone numbers.
  • Call records.
  • Location information.
  • Internet usage data.
  • Subscriber identities.
  • LCommunications metadata.

These activities are governed by data protection laws alongside telecommunications confidentiality and communications secrecy requirements.

Children's privacy and online safety

Children require particular protection because they may not fully understand how their personal information is collected and used.

Important legislation includes:

  • GDPR provisions concerning children.
  • US Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA).
  • UK Age Appropriate Design Code.
  • National child-protection and online safety laws.
  • Specific provisions of other national privacy laws.

Common requirements

Depending on the applicable law, organizations may need to:

  • Obtain parental authorization for certain processing.
  • Provide child-friendly privacy notices.
  • Restrict targeted advertising.
  • Avoid unnecessary collection of children's data.
  • Apply stronger default privacy settings.
  • Limit profiling.
  • Provide age-appropriate safeguards.

For example, under GDPR Article 8, the default age at which a child can independently consent to certain information society services is 16, but EU member states can lower it to 13.

artificial intelligence and privacy law

AI creates significant privacy challenges because it can process enormous amounts of information, identify patterns, infer personal characteristics, and make predictions about individuals.

Main privacy risks associated with AI

  • Collection of personal information without appropriate legal grounds.
  • Training AI models using personal information.
  • Unauthorized disclosure of confidential information.
  • Automated profiling.
  • Biased decisions.
  • Facial recognition.
  • Biometric surveillance.
  • Inaccurate personal information.
  • Re-identification of supposedly anonymous information.
  • Cross-border transfers of personal information.

Important regulations

GDPR

Applies to personal data processed through AI systems. It includes rules on lawful processing, transparency, profiling, and certain automated decisions.

EU AI Act

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act regulates AI systems according to their risk levels. It is not itself a general privacy law, but it overlaps with data protection obligations.

Swiss FADP

Applies when AI systems process personal data within its scope. Relevant obligations include transparency, proportionality, security, and rules on automated individual decisions.

US legislation

AI privacy requirements arise from federal and state privacy laws, consumer protection rules, and sector-specific legislation.

Example: AI recruitment

Suppose a company uses AI to analyze job applications.

The system:

  • Reads CVs.
  • Analyzes applicants' experience.
  • Scores candidates.
  • Predicts suitability.
  • Recommends whom to interview.

This can involve personal data processing, profiling, potential discrimination, and automated decision-making concerns.

The company must assess the applicable privacy and employment laws, explain relevant processing, establish a legal basis, and provide appropriate safeguards.

Privacy compliance: What businesses must do

For organizations, privacy compliance should be an ongoing management process, not simply the creation of a privacy policy.

Important compliance documents

Organizations may need some or all of the following:

Document Purpose
Privacy policy Explains personal data processing.
Record of Processing Activities (ROPA) Documents processing operations.
Data Processing Agreement (DPA) Defines processor responsibilities.
Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) Evaluates high-risk processing.
Data retention policy Establishes retention and deletion rules.
Information security policy Establishes security requirements.
Data breach response plan Defines incident handling.
Cookie policy Explains cookie usage.
International transfer agreements Establish safeguards for international data transfers.
Employee privacy policy Explains workplace data processing.

Not every organization is legally required to maintain every document. Requirements depend on the relevant jurisdiction, organization, and processing activities.

what can regulators do?

Depending on their legal powers, they can:

  • Investigate complaints.
  • Request documents and information.
  • Conduct inspections.
  • Order organizations to change processing practices.
  • Require certain information to be deleted.
  • Restrict or prohibit certain processing.
  • Issue administrative fines.
  • Refer criminal matters to appropriate authorities.
  • Publish enforcement decisions.

How can an individual file a privacy complaint?

A typical process is:

  1. Identify the organization responsible for processing your information.
  2. Contact its privacy department or data protection officer.
  3. Explain the suspected violation.
  4. Request an explanation, correction, restriction, or deletion where appropriate.
  5. Keep copies of correspondence and relevant evidence.
  6. Contact the competent supervisory authority if the matter is unresolved.

In Switzerland, the relevant federal authority is the FDPIC.

The future of privacy law

Privacy legislation will continue evolving as technology changes.

Some of the most important emerging areas are:

  • Biometric privacy
    Facial recognition, fingerprints, voice recognition, and other identifying technologies.
  • Surveillance and tracking
    Government surveillance, workplace monitoring, smart cities, and location tracking.
  • Generative AI and machine learning
    AI training datasets, personal information in prompts, profiling, and automated decisions.
  • Internet of Things (IoT)
    Smart watches, connected vehicles, home assistants, and other devices continuously collecting data.
  • Quantum computing
    Future implications for encryption, cybersecurity, and the protection of stored personal information.
  • Digital identity
    Electronic identification, digital credentials, identity verification, and control over personal information.

What you should remember

The entire subject can be summarized in five fundamental questions:

  1. Collection: Is an organization legally allowed to collect this information?
  2. Use: Is it allowed to use the information for this particular purpose?
  3. Disclosure: Is it allowed to share the information with other people or organizations?
  4. Protection: Is it taking sufficient steps to protect the information?
  5. Control: Can individuals exercise their legal rights over their personal information?

If an organization cannot satisfactorily answer these questions, it may have a privacy compliance problem.
