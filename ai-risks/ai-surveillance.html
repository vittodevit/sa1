Risks of AI - Surveillance

AI-Powered Surveillance and Facial Recognition

A camera used to record an event. With AI, it can recognise a face, follow a person across a city and flag behaviour in real time. The shift from recording to continuous identification is what makes surveillance a privacy problem at scale.

AI risks Privacy critical By Giulio Finocchiaro

On this page

From recording to recognising

Traditional CCTV stores video for a human to review after an incident. AI-powered surveillance adds models that analyse the video as it arrives: detecting people, classifying objects, reading faces and linking sightings into a movement history.

The important change is scale. A police officer can watch one screen; a model can process thousands of camera feeds at once, every hour of the day, without being asked to. When the output is a list of names, anonymity in public space becomes conditional on the accuracy and the rules of the system.

How the pipeline works

A facial recognition system is a chain of models. Each step introduces its own errors, and an error early in the chain cannot be repaired later.

  1. Step 01 · Capture

    The camera records a scene

    Resolution, angle, lighting and compression all affect the quality of the face that can be extracted.

  2. Step 02 · Detection

    Faces are located

    A detector finds faces and landmarks in the frame. Blurred, partially hidden or very small faces may be missed entirely.

  3. Step 03 · Embedding

    The face becomes a vector

    A neural network converts the face into a numerical template. Similar faces should produce nearby vectors.

  4. Step 04 · Matching

    The vector is compared

    In a 1:N search, the template is compared with a database or watchlist. A similarity threshold decides whether a match is proposed.

  5. Step 05 · Action

    A human receives an alert

    The system flags a candidate. Whether the alert is accurate, and what happens next, depends on the review process around the model.

Detection is not identification

Counting people in a crowd or detecting a person in a restricted area can often be done without recognising anyone. Identification is a separate, much more sensitive step that requires a database of faces and a legal basis.

Where it is used

The same technology can be deployed for very different purposes. The context, the legal basis and the review process decide whether a use is proportionate.

Common deployments and their main risks
Context What AI adds Main risk
Public CCTV Real-time face matching and tracking across cameras Mass identification
Retail analytics Counting visitors and estimating demographics or attention Inference without consent
Border control Automated 1:N checks against watchlists False matches and redress
Criminal investigations Image search across scraped or police databases Bias and function creep
Access control Face unlock for a known, consenting group Limited if local

Accuracy and bias

Face recognition has improved quickly, but "accurate on average" is not enough in surveillance. A false match can lead to a stop, an arrest or a public accusation. A missed match can leave a person on a watchlist unnoticed.

Error rates are not the same for everyone

NIST's Face Recognition Vendor Test found large differences in false positive rates across demographic groups in many algorithms, with the highest error rates often for women, older people and people with darker skin tones. A system deployed without demographic testing can reproduce and amplify existing inequalities.

The quality of the input matters as much as the model. A camera looking up at a crowd, a compressed stream or a face turned away produce worse templates. Systems should report performance under realistic conditions, not only on clean benchmark photos.

Human review is not a magic fix

If an operator sees a suggested match, they tend to trust it. Automation bias can turn a low-confidence alert into a decision. Reviewers need independent evidence, clear uncertainty information and the authority to say no.

Consent and anonymity

Walking through a public square is not the same as agreeing to be identified. Surveillance systems often process biometric data without meaningful consent, and the people affected may never know that a template was created or how long it is kept.

The effects go beyond the individual alert. When people expect to be recognised, they may avoid protests, clinics, places of worship or political meetings. This chilling effect weakens public life even when no one is ever matched incorrectly.

A breach of a face database cannot be undone

Unlike a password, a face cannot be reset. A leaked template or a scraped image database creates a permanent risk of re-identification, and it can be reused for purposes that were never disclosed. Retention limits, encryption and on-device matching reduce the exposure, but they do not make the data harmless.

The legal picture is fragmented. The GDPR treats biometric identification as special-category data, and the EU AI Act bans real-time remote biometric identification in publicly accessible spaces, with narrow exceptions for law enforcement. Other countries have different rules, which matters when the technology and the data cross borders.

Safeguards

What responsible deployment requires

  • Necessity and proportionality: prove that a less invasive method cannot achieve the same legitimate goal.
  • Clear legal basis: define the purpose, the data involved and the people affected before the system is installed.
  • Independent oversight: audits, published policies and a route for complaints and redress.
  • Retention limits: delete templates and footage when the purpose is fulfilled, not "just in case".
  • Accuracy testing by group: measure false matches across demographics, lighting and camera conditions.
  • Transparency: visible notices, impact assessments and meaningful information for the public.

Frequently asked questions

Can a mask or makeup defeat face recognition?

Sometimes. Occlusion reduces accuracy and is a known weakness of many systems. Systems that claim to work through masks need dedicated testing, because a confident wrong match is more dangerous than a clear failure.

Is public CCTV with face recognition always illegal?

No, but it is tightly restricted. In the EU, real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces is generally prohibited, with narrow exceptions. Other jurisdictions have their own rules, and legality does not automatically make a deployment proportionate or fair.

The question is not only "does it work?"

A surveillance system can be technically accurate and still be unjust. Consent, purpose, oversight and the possibility of anonymity are part of the security question, not obstacles to it.
