Digital Footprints, Cookies and Trackers

What is a Digital Footprint?

A digital footprint is the trail of data we leave behind whenever we go online. It grows with almost every online activity: posting on social media, shopping and banking online, using mobile apps and smart devices such as voice assistants and fitness trackers, or simply browsing the web. Our footprint matters because many people can see parts of it: employers, schools and landlords may run background checks, companies track our browsing to target ads and sometimes to personalise prices, governments store certain data for legal or security reasons, and cybercriminals look for leaked details to commit fraud or identity theft. A large footprint can therefore affect our reputation, our privacy and our security. It is practically impossible to erase a digital footprint completely: deleting an account helps, but copies of our data may survive in backups, archives or third-party databases, and public posts can be saved or screenshotted long after they have been deleted.

Active and Passive Footprints

An active digital footprint is created by actions we take ourselves, such as publishing posts, leaving comments, sharing photos and videos, sending emails or creating accounts. A passive digital footprint is collected indirectly, without any deliberate action on our part: browsing history, search queries, location data, device information and IP addresses. Passive traces are much harder to notice and to manage, because we usually do not know when, or by whom, they are being collected.

Cookies

Cookies are small pieces of data that a website stores in our browser so that it can recognise us when it reads them back later. They serve useful purposes, such as keeping us logged in, remembering the items in a shopping cart and filling in forms automatically, but they are also used to track what we view and to show us targeted ads. There are four main types. First-party cookies are created by the website we are visiting. Third-party cookies are created by other companies, usually advertisers, and are embedded in ads, banners, videos and social media buttons; because the same company can appear on many websites, these cookies can follow us from site to site. Session cookies are deleted when we leave a site, whereas persistent cookies remain until they expire and can be used to build a profile of our interests. In Europe, websites must ask for our consent before using non-essential cookies, which is why cookie banners appear so often. Many browsers, including Safari and Firefox, now block third-party cookies by default.

Tracking Pixels

A tracking pixel is a tiny transparent image, usually just 1×1 pixel, hidden in a website, an email or an advertisement. When the page or email loads, the pixel sends information back to a server, such as the pages visited, the ads clicked, and the user's IP address, operating system and approximate location. Retargeting pixels follow users after they leave a website so that its ads can be shown to them elsewhere, while conversion pixels measure whether an ad led to a purchase or a sign-up. In emails, pixels reveal when a message has been opened. Pixels are harder to control than cookies: they are invisible, they are usually disclosed only in privacy policies, users cannot simply delete or disable them, and they can track people across different devices.

Browser Fingerprinting

Browser fingerprinting identifies a user by collecting many small details about their browser and device and combining them into a unique "fingerprint". These details can be quite ordinary: screen resolution, time zone, operating system version, installed fonts and even remaining battery life. More advanced techniques ask the browser to draw a hidden image (canvas fingerprinting) or to process a sound (audio fingerprinting), then measure the tiny differences caused by the hardware. On smartphones, apps can do something similar, known as device fingerprinting. Fingerprinting is usually carried out by third-party trackers present on many websites, so a single tracker can follow the same person across all of them. Unlike a cookie, a fingerprint is not stored on the device, so there is nothing to delete, and private or incognito mode does not stop it. To be useful to trackers, a fingerprint must be both unique and stable over time; in 2010 the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) found that the vast majority of browsers met both conditions. Anyone can check their own browser with the EFF's free Cover Your Tracks test.
