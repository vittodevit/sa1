Data Brokers, Data Mining and Data Selling

What are Data Brokers?

Data brokers are companies that collect personal information about people they have no direct relationship with and then sell it to other businesses. In 2014 the US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) published a study of nine data brokers, including Acxiom, CoreLogic, Datalogix, Intelius and PeekYou. It found that together they held billions of data elements covering nearly every American consumer: one broker had information on more than 1.4 billion consumer transactions and 700 billion data elements, while another added more than 3 billion new data points every month. The scale has continued to grow: in 2021 Acxiom advertised more than 11,000 data attributes per person for about 2.5 billion consumers worldwide. Because these companies never deal directly with the people in their databases, most people do not even know they exist.

Where Does the Data Come From?

Data brokers gather information from both online and offline sources, mostly without people's knowledge. These include public records such as property registers, purchase data from shops, warranty registrations, magazine subscriptions, social media activity and the websites and apps people use. Some of this data is highly sensitive: a visit to a website about depression, the purchase of a specific medical product or the installation of a religious app can all end up in a profile. Data also passes from one broker to another; in the FTC study, seven of the nine companies had shared information with another broker, so the same data can travel through many hands.

Data Mining and Profiling

Data mining means searching very large datasets for patterns that can be used to predict behaviour. A single source usually reveals only a few facts about a person, but combining many sources produces a detailed profile. This profile contains actual data, which is collected directly, and derived data, which is inferred from patterns: for example, predicting that a customer is pregnant from what they buy, or that someone is likely to miss a loan payment. People are then grouped into segments. Some are harmless, such as "Big Spender Parents", but others concern sensitive topics: the FTC found segments about health conditions such as pregnancy, diabetes and high cholesterol, as well as segments like "Urban Scramble" and "Rural Everlasting", based on ethnicity, income and age. Finally, brokers create scores that predict how a person is likely to behave, which companies can use to make automated decisions.

Collecting, Analyzing and Selling

The data broker business works in three steps. First, raw data is collected from many sources. Next, it is analysed to build profiles, segments and scores that describe and predict people's behaviour. Finally, the results are sold. The FTC identified three main kinds of product: marketing products, which help companies target advertising; risk mitigation products, which help companies verify customers and prevent fraud, and which can determine whether a transaction is approved; and people search products, which allow anyone to look up information about a specific person. The same data can also be used in unexpected ways: the FTC noted that a category such as "Biker Enthusiasts" could be used to offer motorcycle discounts, but an insurer might also read it as a sign of risky behaviour.

Legal and Ethical Concerns

The FTC concluded that the data broker industry suffers from a fundamental lack of transparency. It asked the US Congress for laws that would allow people to see the data held about them, correct it and opt out of its sale, and that would require explicit consent before sensitive data, such as health information, is collected. In Europe the GDPR applies, yet data trading still raises problems. In 2024 German journalists investigating the data marketplace Datarade were offered 3.6 billion location data points from Germany free of charge; the data concerned millions of people, who could be identified from their movement patterns. Under the GDPR, the consent a person gives to the website that first collects their data does not extend to its resale to unknown third parties, and most people do not expect their data to be sold for purposes they know nothing about. Some jurisdictions are trying to increase transparency: California, for instance, requires data brokers to register with a state agency and offers a single procedure for requesting deletion. Ethical concerns remain as well, including discrimination based on profiles and the sale of sensitive information. Privacy laws are discussed in more detail on the Privacy Laws and Regulations page.
