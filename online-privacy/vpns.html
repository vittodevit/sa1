Cybersecurity & Privacy

VPNs

VPN's definition

A VPN (virtual private network) is software and a network service that creates an encrypted connection between your device and a VPN server.
It improves privacy and security on the path between you and that server, and it makes websites see the VPN server’s IP address rather than yours (but it is not anonymity software and does not make unlawful conduct lawful).

What a VPN is

A VPN is a “virtual private network”: it creates a private-looking communications channel across a public network such as your home broadband, mobile data, hotel Wi‑Fi, or office network.

It is used for two broad purposes:

  • Personal privacy/security:
    protecting traffic on untrusted Wi‑Fi, reducing what an ISP can observe, and hiding your IP address from websites.
  • Organizational access:
    allowing employees to connect securely to a company network, internal applications, or remote systems.

In practice, “VPN” usually means a commercial consumer VPN service, but the same technology is also used by employers, governments, and IT departments for secure remote access.

How it works

When you activate a VPN, its app establishes an authenticated, encrypted connection (a “tunnel”) from your device to a server operated by the VPN provider.

  1. Your device encrypts traffic before it leaves your network.
  2. Your ISP, internet service provider, or the operator of public Wi‑Fi sees an encrypted connection to the VPN server, rather than the individual destinations you visit.
  3. The VPN server decrypts the traffic and forwards it to the website or service.
  4. The destination sees the VPN server’s IP address, not your home or mobile IP address.
  5. Replies return through the same encrypted tunnel to your device.

Modern VPNs rely on cryptographic protocols such as WireGuard, OpenVPN, and IKEv2/IPsec.
Encryption is intended to provide confidentiality, integrity, and authentication of the connection.

What it potects

A VPN can meaningfully reduce certain risks:

  • Local-network snooping:
    It makes it much harder for someone on the same café, hotel, or airport Wi‑Fi to observe the contents of your unencrypted traffic.
  • ISP visibility of destinations:
    Your ISP can generally see that you use a VPN and how much data you transfer, but not which individual sites you visit through the tunnel.
  • IP-based location exposure:
    Websites normally see the VPN server’s IP address, which can make your apparent location different from your real one.
  • Some censorship or geo-restrisctions:
    By appearing to connect from another country, a VPN may allow access to services that restrict access by IP location; whether this complies with the service’s terms is a separate issue.

Important limitations

It is not anonymity

A VPN changes the IP address seen by websites, but it does not erase your digital identity.
If you sign in to Google, Facebook, a shopping account, or a streaming service, that company can still associate activity with your account.

Other tracking methods continue to work, including cookies, tracking pixels, browser and device fingerprinting, mobile advertising identifiers, and GPS/location permissions.

The provider becomes a trust point

Your traffic is encrypted to the VPN server, but the VPN provider can see the destination of your traffic after decrypting it.
A provider with weak security, poor privacy practices, intrusive logging, advertising injection, malware, or data-selling practices can undermine the privacy you expected.

A “no-logs” claim is only as strong as the provider’s actual architecture, audit practices, jurisdiction, and history.
If a provider retains identifying information, authorities in its jurisdiction may be able to compel disclosure of what it holds.

It does not stop malware or phishing

A VPN does not inspect every malicious link, prevent you from installing malware, or stop you from entering a password into a fraudulent website.
It protects the transport of traffic; it is not a substitute for antivirus, updates, password managers, multi-factor authentication, or careful browsing.

It can reduce speed and reliability

Because traffic takes an additional route through a VPN server and must be encrypted and decrypted, latency and throughput can suffer.
The effect varies with distance to the server, server load, protocol, and the quality of your underlying connection.

It may not cover everything

Some VPN configurations use “split tunneling,” sending selected apps or destinations outside the encrypted tunnel.
Also, only traffic actually routed through the VPN is protected; DNS requests, system updates, or particular applications may behave differently depending on the setup.

Common misconceptions

Misconception Reality
"A VPN makes me anonymous." It hides your IP address from destinations and encrypts traffic in transit, but accounts, cookies, fingerprinting, GPS, and the VPN provider itself can still identify or correlate activity.
"A VPN makes illegal activity legal." It does not.
Copyright infringement, fraud, harassment, hacking, and other unlawful acts remain unlawful; a VPN may merely make attribution harder.
"A VPN prevents all tracking." It does not prevent cookies, logged-in accounts, fingerprinting, ad IDs, or other tracking techniques.
"A VPN guarantees safety on public Wi-Fi." It protects traffic routed through it, but does not protect against phishing, malicious downloads, compromised devices, or fake Wi‑Fi networks designed to steal credentials.
"A VPN always bypasses every restriction." Services can detect VPN IPs, require payment methods or identity checks, enforce account-country rules, or block known VPN address ranges.
"Free VPNs are equivalent to paid ones." Free services may monetize through ads, data collection, limited servers, or weak privacy practices; the business model matters.

Legal status: the key principle

In most countries, using a VPN is legal .
The legal question is usually not "Did you use a VPN?" but "What did you do while using it?" A VPN does not create an exemption from criminal law, copyright law, sanctions, data-protection rules, gambling rules, or a website's terms of service.

There are three distinct legal layers:

  • Use of the technology:
    Legal in many jurisdictions, including Switzerland, the EU, the US, the UK, Canada, and Australia.
  • The activity conducted through it:
    Illegal conduct remains illegal, whether or not a VPN was used.
  • Circumvention of state controls:
    In some countries, using an unapproved VPN (or using one to evade censorship) can itself be restricted or penalized.

How rules differe by country

Jurisdiction General position Important nuance
Switzerland VPN use is legal for personal and business purposes. Swiss ISPs retain certain connection metadata for six months; a VPN can conceal destinations from the ISP, but the fact and timing of a VPN connection may still be recorded.
Bypassing blocks on unlicensed gambling sites can create legal exposure.
European Union / UK / US / Canada / Australia VPNs are generally lawful and widely used as cybersecurity tools. Laws still apply to the underlying conduct; streaming platforms may prohibit VPN use in their terms.
China Only government-approved VPN services are lawful; unauthorized VPN use can lead to warnings, fines, device confiscation, or detention in serious cases. The restriction concerns unapproved international connections and circumvention tools, not merely the abstract existence of VPN technology.
Russia VPN technology is not categorically banned, but providers must comply with blocking requirements; services that refuse can be blocked. Since 2024, sharing information about circumvention tools can also be restricted, and corporate use is subject to specific conditions.
Iran, North Korea, and some other states VPN use may be prohibited, tightly controlled, or criminalized. Enforcement and the precise legal status can be opaque and change quickly; travelers should verify current local rules before departure.

Practical guidance

For someone in Switzerland, a VPN is legal and can be useful on public Wi-Fi or when you want to reduce the destinations visible to your ISP.
But it will not make you anonymous, will not override Swiss law, and does not remove the contractual or legal consequences of using it to evade restrictions.

When choosing one, prioritize:

  • A clear, specific privacy policy and a credible no-logs approach.
  • Independent security audits and transparent ownership/jurisdiction.
  • Modern protocols such as WireGuard or OpenVPN, rather than obsolete or obscure protocols.
  • A kill switch, DNS-leak protection, and clear handling of IPv6 and WebRTC.
  • Avoidance of free services with vague data-sharing or advertising practices.
